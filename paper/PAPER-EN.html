Multiplicative correlations in the Goldbach error field

An empirical study

Goldbach Error Field Project

Abstract. Let G(N) be a smooth weighted balanced Goldbach count, e(N)=c_φ𝔖(N)−G(N) its deviation from the Hardy–Littlewood main term, and ε(N)=e(N)/(c_φ𝔖(N)) the relative error, whose standard deviation is 0.086 at N≈3×10⁴ (i.e. of the order of the pair-counting noise). We report a reproducible empirical regularity of the field ε: its two-point correlation across multiplicative dilations,

c(k) = corr( ε(N), ε(kN) ),      N in a window of all even integers,

is (i) sign definite: sign c(k) = (−1)^{ω(k)} for 28 of the 31 integers 2 ≤ k ≤ 49 tested, the only significant exception being k=26 at 2σ; (ii) of magnitude ≈0.135 at primes, but not constant: the nine prime values span −0.158 … −0.101 with χ² = 72.8 on 8 degrees of freedom (p = 1.4×10⁻¹²); (iii) approximately multiplicative for coprime pairs (k₁,k₂)=1 (6 of 10 tests within 1σ, the remaining four at 1.1σ, 2.4σ, 3.2σ and 4.0σ; three of the six are prime-square cases, so we do not claim squarefreeness); (iv) not given by the completely multiplicative continuation at prime powers (c(p²) is negative, 3–13σ away from c(p)² (7–13σ for p ≤ 5)), so the "Liouville-type" reading c(k)=α^{ω(k)}λ(k) is quantitatively rejected; and (v) present at every prime ratio from 10⁴ to 10⁹, with no decay law resolved: the window-to-window scatter (±0.025) is three to four times the within-window error, which no single-window quotation reflects.

The measurement is not a consequence of the normalisation (for k=2 it coincides with corr(G(N),G(2N))), of the weight shape (five different weights), of the position in N (the effect survives inside blocks of width 40), of the factorisation of N, of a random-prime model (−0.017±0.025), or of any smooth trend.

The field is, however, not white, and this is a separate regularity: its autocorrelation is zero at lags 2, 4, 10, 20, 200, 1000 and positive at lags that share small-prime factors, growing with the number of the first five primes dividing the lag and independent of its size — +0.064 at k = 210, +0.117 at k = 2310, +0.129 at k = 4620, and about zero for lags of the same magnitude without that structure (§5.10). The corresponding error bars are larger than 1/sqrt(M) would give.

We also record a structural obstruction that makes the measurement surprising: G(N) uses primes in [N/3,2N/3] while G(kN) uses [kN/3,2kN/3], and for k=2 these intervals are disjoint; moreover the four-point local densities of the four linear forms (n, N−n, n′, 2N−n′) factorise exactly at every prime. Hence any model of the primes with the correct local densities and independent positions predicts c(k)=0. The measured −0.14 is therefore either a residual estimator effect that we have not been able to locate, or a long-range four-point correlation of the primes. Section 5.6 states a decisive test that separates the two.

We do not establish any theorem, and nothing here bears on a proof of the binary Goldbach conjecture. Appendix A records a technical review of the recent conditional preprint of Li–Liu: we verify its exact operator identity and one of its constants, and note the single display slip we can find (a missing conjugation bar in the Hermitian-symmetry line, which does not affect the argument).

Keywords. Goldbach conjecture; weighted count; Hardy–Littlewood singular series; multiplicative correlation; Liouville function; empirical field theory.

1. Introduction

1.1 The object

Fix an even N ≥ 4 and a weight φ ∈ C_c^∞(ℝ), supp φ ⊂ [1/3,2/3], 0 ≤ φ ≤ 1, φ(u) = φ(1−u). Write

G(N) = (2/N) Σ_{N/3<n<N/2} Λ(n)Λ(N−n) φ(n/N)φ((N−n)/N)                (1.1)
c_φ  = ∫_0^1 φ²(u) du                                                (1.2)
𝔖(N) = 2C₂ ∏_{p|N, p>2} (p−1)/(p−2),   C₂ = ∏_{p>2}(1−1/(p−1)²)       (1.3)
e(N) = c_φ𝔖(N) − G(N),     ε(N) = e(N)/(c_φ𝔖(N))                     (1.4)

Throughout, unless stated otherwise, φ(u) = cos²(3π(u−1/2)), so that φ² = cos⁴ and c_φ = ∫cos⁴ = 1/8 = 0.125. (The constant 0.091146 = ∫cos⁸ belongs to the weight φ = cos⁴, not to φ² = cos⁴; it enters only inside a positive normalising factor, so no numerical result depends on the distinction.)

The sum in (1.1) is over the half-open range N/3 < n < N/2, so each pair is counted once and the diagonal term n = m = N/2 is excluded by definition. That is the convention used by every script in Appendix B; including the diagonal instead changes every c(k) by at most 0.002 and sd(ε) by 0.5%, i.e. by less than a third of the quoted errors, so the choice is immaterial here but is stated rather than left implicit.

Because φ is symmetric, G(N) counts ordered pairs of prime powers that are balanced: both members lie in [N/3,2N/3]. Hardy–Littlewood predicts G(N) ≈ c_φ𝔖(N).

The empirical distribution of e(N) is Gaussian (Section 3.2) with

sd(e) ≈ 0.33 (log N)/√N,     i.e.   sd(ε) ≈ 0.086  at N ≈ 3×10⁴   (1.5)

which is of the order of the pair-counting (Poisson) scale, but the coefficient is not pinned down by a single number. At N = 1.2×10⁴ the support [N/3,2N/3] contains 98 prime-pair centres — 196 ordered representations, but the sum runs over n < N/2, so each pair is one term — giving a naive Poisson reference 1/√98 = 0.101; the varying weight raises it to ≈0.143, since the effective count is (Σw)²/Σw² = 98·E[w]²/E[w²] ≈ 49 with E[w²]/E[w]² = 2.0. The measured 0.086 is below both, i.e. the field is mildly sub-Poisson (variance 0.72× the naive reference), the same sieve-type suppression found in §5.7(3). We therefore use the Poisson scale only as an order of magnitude. So ε is, to first order, noise; everything below concerns the structure of that noise.

1.2 What is measured

c(k) := corr( ε(N), ε(kN) )                                         (1.6)

where corr is the Pearson correlation over N in a window of consecutive even integers, and ε(kN) is computed from the definition (1.1)–(1.4) at the argument kN (so k must be a positive integer; the window of kN is k times the window of N).

For k=2 the statistic is canonical: since N is even, 𝔖(2N)=𝔖(N), hence

c(2) = corr( G(N), G(2N) )                                          (1.7)

with no normalisation freedom whatsoever. All other k differ from this only by the smooth factor log N/√N and the singular-series ratio 𝔖(kN)/𝔖(N), which equals 1 or (k−1)/(k−2) according to whether k | N. We verified that all reasonable normalisations give the same c(k) to within 0.005 (Section 5.1).

1.4 What this paper does not do

It does not prove anything about Goldbach's conjecture, it does not identify a mechanism, and it does not claim that c(k) is a multiplicative function. It reports a measurement, the controls that survive, the descriptions that fail, and one structural reason to be surprised. Section 5.6 is the part we consider most useful: it turns the observation into a falsifiable question about four-point correlations of the primes.

2. The estimator and its calibration

2.1 Conventions

All arithmetic is in float64. Λ is generated by an Eratosthenes sieve up to 2.6×10⁶ (or higher where kN requires it); 𝔖(N) by factorisation with a smallest-prime-factor table; 2C₂ = 1.320323632…. The weight is evaluated exactly (cos⁴), not from a grid (a 10⁵-point table with index truncation changes G by at most 3×10⁻⁵ relative) (§2.2).

2.2 Two independent implementations

The measurements in §4 were produced by code written from scratch, independent of every earlier implementation:

  • lab/tool.py — vectorised NumPy; lab/ngb.py — JIT (numba) version, 13× faster;
  • lab/fast2.py — multi-process driver, python fast2.py --n0 12000 --m 40000 --k 2,3,6.

Cross-checks: our two implementations agree to 1.5×10⁻¹⁵ relative. Against an implementation that evaluates the weight on a 10⁵-point grid with index truncation, the weighted counts differ by 3×10⁻⁶ … 3×10⁻⁵ relative (a 3×10⁻⁶ error in u, amplified by the steepness of cos⁴); the correlation coefficients are unaffected at the 10⁻³ level. The archived c(2)=−0.1403, c(3)=−0.1230, c(6)=+0.0255 at M=15000 are reproduced digit for digit, as are the two-point table of §2.5 and the distribution table of §3.2.

(ii) A split-half comparison is weaker than it looks. Two halves at M = 15000 give −0.084/−0.110; a half-sample has se ≈ 0.008, so the spread is 3σ, not "stable". Our own split halves differ by 0.013 at M=4×10⁴ (for k=2).

2.4 A null model

Replace the primes by a random set: n is "prime" independently with probability 1/log n for odd n, nothing for even n, and normalise G by its exact expectation E[G](N) = 2Σ p(n)p(N−n)φ²(n/N) (so that the smooth trend is removed analytically). Eight independent draws give

c(2) = +0.038, −0.054, +0.047, −0.148, −0.063, +0.087, −0.041, −0.000
mean = −0.017,  sd of the mean = 0.025

consistent with zero. Random primes do not reproduce the effect. This null also fixes the calibration of the estimator: the ratio G/E[G] is unbiased, and its fluctuation in N is white.

2.6 Remark: the coprimality condition (nm,N)=1

The object of the Li–Liu identity (Appendix A) carries the restriction (nm,N)=1, which (1.1) omits. On the support this restriction is vacuous: Λ(n) ≠ 0 forces n = p^a, so (n,N) ≠ 1 means p | N and n = p^a ∈ [N/3,2N/3]. Such n are: N/2 when N = 2p^a (a handful of N in any window), and, for the other divisors of N in the support, the prime powers p^a with p|N. A direct computation gives, for N = 12000, 12002, 16384, 13122, 31250,

G with (nm,N)=1  =  G without the restriction,   to machine precision.

So the empirical object of this paper is the object of the operator identity, up to O(N⁻¹).

4. The correlation field

4.0 Two window conventions, used in different tables

The window enters every number in this paper, and two different conventions are used, which is a defect of presentation rather than of measurement but must be stated.

  • Fixed count. [N_0, N_0 + 2M]: M consecutive even integers, so the relative width shrinks as N_0 grows. This is the convention in §3.1, in the scale scans of §4.10, and in §5.8–§5.11 (the calibrated sieve null, the omega-memory, and the zero component all use M consecutive even integers starting at N_0).
  • Fixed relative width. [N_0, 1.3 N_0]: always 30% of N_0. This is the convention stated in §4.1 for the c(k) tables.

The two are not interchangeable. At N_0 = 2×10^5 a fixed-count window with M = 2000 spans 2% of N_0 while [N_0,1.3N_0] spans 30% — a factor of fifteen. c(k) depends on the window (§4.8 shows a 0.031 spread across the five weight shapes alone, and the omega-memory of §5.10 depends on N), so no table in this paper should be compared with another without checking which convention it uses; where a number is quoted in two places with different conventions, the difference is a real difference and is reported as such.

4.3 S1: the sign law (established)

Twenty-eight of the thirty-one values obey

sign c(k) = (−1)^{ω(k)}                                               (4.1)

with ω(k) the number of distinct prime factors. The three exceptions are c(26) = −0.0144 (2σ), c(28) = −0.0014 (0.2σ) and c(30) = +0.0027 (0.4σ); only the first is statistically significant. In particular the sign is not that of λ(k): for k = 4, 9, 25, 49 one has ω(k)=1 and λ(k)=+1, and c is negative in all four cases. The law (4.1) is the most robust statement in this paper: it holds for five different weight shapes (§4.8) and in seven different scale windows (§4.10).

How much does 28/31 mean? If the 31 signs were independent fair coins, P(X >= 28) = 2.3×10⁻⁶ (and P(X >= 27) = 1.7×10⁻⁵). They are not independent — all 31 correlations are computed from the same field — so this is an indication of scale, not a test. What it rules out is a coin-flip origin; what it does not supply is a calibrated significance, which only the block bootstrap of §4.2 and the sieve null of §5.8 can provide.

4.7 A further logical constraint: rational ratios

Multiplicativity on ℕ together with the symmetry c(1/k) = c(k) forces c(p/q) = c(p)c(q) for all primes p, q. In particular

c(3/2) = c(2)c(3) = c(6) = c(4/6),

so the three measurements must coincide. Measured (archive run, M = 2×10⁴, the same windows as §4.2): c(3/2) = +0.0140, c(6) = +0.0235 (this one is also in the table of §4.2), c(4/6) = +0.0478 — a spread of 0.034 at the 3–4σ level. Earlier drafts stated the identity alone; the numerical spread is what makes it a test. This is the same phenomenon as the failure of §4.5, seen on the rational lattice; it shows that whatever c is, it is not a multiplicative function on ℚ_{>0}. We therefore state all results for integer k only.

  • they do establish that the pairing channel is homogeneous in N at fixed scale, and that the local mass is not the driver;
  • they cannot exclude a density-channel component, because that component is common to all ten windows;
  • and they cannot reduce the between-scale uncertainty, because at this statistic the "scale" and the "sample" are the same axis: to change the primes one must change N by O(N), which changes the scale by O(1).

The only defensible summary is c(2) ≈ −0.07 with a scatter of ±0.03 that no measurement in hand resolves — the statistical error at each scale (0.006) is five times smaller than the scale-to-scale spread, and the two cannot be separated by construction.

4.12 Not the explicit formula: the zeros build a smooth field, not a white one

The classical reading of a Goldbach error is that it is driven by the zeros of ζ through the explicit formula. That reading is testable directly, and it fails on the statistics rather than on a number.

We located 48 900 ordinates below height 40 000 as sign changes of the Riemann–Siegel main sum, refined by vectorised bisection, and built the two fields the explicit formula can produce on the window N = 12000+2i, i < 15000. Two caveats on the list: the Riemann–von Mangoldt formula predicts 49 395 zeros below 40 000, so about 1% are missed where the remainder of the Riemann–Siegel formula flips the sign (refining the search grid from 0.2 to 0.005 changes the count from 48 533 to 48 901 and then stops), and the ordinates are those of the main sum, so they carry an error of O(0.4) at the first zero and ≈0.01 near t = 4×10⁴. Neither affects the conclusion below, which is about the smoothness of the resulting field:

P(N) = |L(N)|² = Σ_{j,k} N^{i(γ_j+γ_k)}/(γ_j γ_k)   (two zeros: pair-count-like)

with real positive coefficients — the most favourable choice, since any phase assignment only adds cancellation. Then

The reason is structural: the explicit formula weights zeros by 1/ρ, so the sum is dominated by the lowest zeros (γ ≈ 14–100), whose phase γ·ΔN/N changes by < 0.02 rad between adjacent N. This does not contradict §5.1(f); it explains it. The zeros control the smooth (density) component of the error, and that component is measured to be ≈1% of the variance (corr(ε,m) = 0.105). The remaining 99% — the pairing noise, which is what carries c(k) — has no zero representation at all. So the effect cannot be the classical zero-driven error term, and the "already known" reading of S8 is closed: the answer to new phenomenon or known implication is not the known implication, whatever else it may be.

5. Why the measurement is surprising

5.1 First, the trivial explanations

(a) Normalisation. For k=2, 𝔖(2N)=𝔖(N) because N is even, so c(2)=corr(G(N),G(2N)) by (1.7); there is no normalisation to blame. For other k we compared five normalisations (σ(N) ∝ √𝔖 log N/√N; constant; √𝔖 only; log N/√N only; 𝔖 log N/√N): the values of c(k) move by at most 0.02 (and by ≤0.006 for k=2), with the sign and the magnitude unchanged.

(b) Position in N. §4.9: the effect survives inside blocks of width 40.

(c) Smooth trend. §1.1: ε is white. corr(ε(N), ε(N+20000)) = +0.009.

(d) Wrong multiplication. corr(ε(N), ε(2N+2)) = +0.038: shifting the double by one even number destroys the effect. So the effect is attached to the exact multiplicative relation, not to the neighbourhood.

(e) Local arithmetic of N. Regressing ε(N) on the indicators p | N (p ≤ 29) and on ω(N) gives R² = 0.0008. Class means of ε(N) by N mod q give (adjusted) R² < 0.02 for q ≤ 4620; removing them leaves c(2) = −0.139 instead of −0.140. A factorisation-dependent field is also excluded by a variance argument, but the argument needs care. For an additive function of the factorisation, ε(N) = Σ_p f_p(v_p(N)), doubling changes only the 2-adic term, so ε(2N) − ε(N) = f₂(v₂(N)) is constant on each v₂ class and Var(ε(2N)−ε(N)) = between-class variance over v₂ = 1,2,3,≥4. A four-valued function has no a priori variance bound, so the content comes from the measurement: the v₂ class means are flat, R² = 0.0004 (§5.7(c)), whereas Var(ε(2N)−ε(N)) = 2.28 σ², i.e. essentially 2σ²(1−c(2)). For a non-additive function of the factorisation the difference need not be a function of v₂ at all, but such a field carries an interaction between the 2-adic and the odd part, and the class-mean fits above (R² = 0.0008 on p | N, p ≤ 29, and on ω(N)) bound precisely that. So ε is not a function of the factorisation of N.

(f) The mass of primes in the interval. The weighted prime mass m(N) = Σ_{N/3<n<2N/3} φ²(n/N)(1_{n prime} − 1/log n) is the only "smooth" channel that can enter ε. We measure corr(ε(N), m(N)) = +0.105, so the mass explains ≈1% of the variance of ε, and at most ≈3% of the covariance c(2). (The quantity corr(m(N),m(2N)) = −0.51 looks spectacular but is not usable: m is smooth on a scale of ≈N/2, and the window contains only about three such scales, so this number has of order one degree of freedom.)

5.2 The obstruction: the two counts use disjoint primes

For k=2 the two sums are

G(N) = 2 Σ_{N/3<n<N/2} Λ(n)Λ(N−n) φ²(n/N) ,      n, N−n ∈ [N/3, 2N/3]     (5.1)
G(2N) = 2 Σ_{2N/3<n<N} Λ(n)Λ(2N−n) φ²(n/(2N)),    n, 2N−n ∈ [2N/3, 4N/3]  (5.2)

and the two prime intervals [N/3,2N/3] and [2N/3,4N/3] are disjoint (they meet at the single point 2N/3, where the weight vanishes to fourth order). The same holds for every k>1: the pairs summing to N straddle N/2 and live in [N/3,2N/3]; the pairs summing to kN straddle kN/2 and live in [kN/3,2kN/3]; these intervals are disjoint as soon as k ≥ 3/2 if the supports are scaled with k as in (1.6).

Hence the two random variables whose correlation we measure are functions of disjoint pieces of the same prime configuration. This is not a convention: it is forced by the arithmetic of n+m=N together with the balanced support.

5.3 The four-point local densities factorise

Write n′ for the variable in (5.2) and consider the four linear forms

n,   N−n,   n′,   2N−n′     (n ∈ [N/3,N/2],  n′ ∈ [2N/3,N]).

By Hardy–Littlewood, the density of quadruples with all four prime is, up to o(1), 𝔖₄/log⁴N with 𝔖₄ = ∏_p σ_p, where σ_p is the number of admissible configurations modulo p divided by the "random" count. For a single pair (n, N−n) the condition is

n ≢ 0 (mod p)  and  n ≢ N (mod p),        i.e. n ∉ {0, N} (mod p),     (5.3)

i.e. p − |{0, N mod p}| admissible residues out of p. For the quadruple the conditions on n and n′ are separate:

n ∉ {0, N} (mod p)   and   n′ ∉ {0, 2N} (mod p).                       (5.4)

Therefore

σ_p(quadruple) = σ_p(pair) · σ_p(pair)      for every prime p,          (5.5)

and the four-point singular series is exactly the product of the two two-point ones. Note that this is not the generic situation: had the two pairs shared a variable (say the same n paired with N−n and with 2N−n), the local counts would not factorise and one would get a positive contribution of the "singular" type. With disjoint pairs and balanced support, (5.5) holds term by term.

Consequently, for any model in which the prime indicator has the correct local densities and independent positions, Cov(G(N),G(2N)) = 0 exactly, and likewise for k>1 after normalisation. Our iid simulation (§2.4) confirms this numerically.

5.4 What this means

The measured c(2) = −0.14 is therefore a deviation of the observed covariance from the factorised prediction, which is exactly zero. Note what 0.14 measures: it is 14% of the standard-deviation product sd(ε(N))·sd(ε(2N)), not 14% of any four-point density — the densities themselves are matched, by §5.3, to machine precision. There are only two possibilities. but not the possibility that the heteroskedasticity of the field inside the window induces a bias of this size: sd(ε(N)) ∝ (log N)/√N varies by a factor 1.7 across [12000,42000], so a Pearson correlation of two such vectors is a weighted average of the local correlations, with the low-N end (where sd(ε) is largest) dominating. Since the weight profile of the low-N end is not the same for ε(N) and ε(2N) (sd(ε(2N))/sd(ε(N)) is itself a function of N), the estimator is not obviously unbiased. This is the most likely residual artifact and should be tested first (§5.6, test T1). (B) A genuine long-range four-point correlation of the primes. Then 𝔖₄ ≪ (1−δ) 𝔖₂𝔖₂ for a positive proportion of configurations with δ ~ 0.14, i.e. the primes behave sub*-independently at large scales, in a way tied to the ratio k of the two sums. This would be a discovery independent of Goldbach, and it would have to be visible in other statistics (e.g. in the variance of G, which would be reduced).

5.6 The decisive test

The covariance can be written as an explicit quadruple sum,

Cov(G(N),G(2N)) = (2/N²) Σ_{n, n′} φ²(n/N) φ²(n′/2N) Λ(n)Λ(N−n)Λ(n′)Λ(2N−n′)
                  − (main terms),                                       (5.6)

and, in the Hardy–Littlewood model, replaced by

(2/N²) Σ_{n, n′} φ² φ² · [𝔖₄(n,N−n;n′,2N−n′) − 𝔖₂(N)𝔖₂(2N)] / log⁴N .   (5.7)

By (5.5) the bracket vanishes identically, so (5.7) = 0, whereas the measured covariance of the two relative errors is c(2)·sd(ε(N))·sd(ε(2N)) ≈ −7×10⁻⁴ at N ≈ 2×10⁴ (with sd(ε(N)) = 0.086 and sd(ε(2N)) ≈ 0.061). The proposed tests are:

  • T1 (estimator). Recompute c(2) with (i) a fixed number of pairs per N (thin the support so that the expected count is constant), (ii) variance-stabilised coordinates (ε(N)/sd(ε(N)) with the exact sd), (iii) a window of constant relative width. If c(2) moves to zero, (A) is confirmed.
  • T2 (four-point sum). This test is vacuous as a test of the physics: by (5.5) the bracket 𝔖₄ − 𝔖₂𝔖₂′ vanishes identically, so a numerical evaluation of Σ_{n,n′}(𝔖₄ − 𝔖₂𝔖₂′)/log⁴N must return zero and can only detect a coding error. That is what §5.7(1) does, and it is a useful check, but it cannot confirm (B). The real test is the one carried out in §5.7(2)–(3): evaluate the empirical four-point sum (5.6) and compare it with a null that has the same local densities.
  • T3 (variance). Under (B) one expects Var(G) to be smaller than the Poisson value. The measured sd(ε) = 0.086 is 0.85× the naive reference (0.101) and 0.60× the weight-reduced one (0.143): mildly sub-Poisson. The sign depends on the convention: using 196 ordered representations instead of 98 centres flips it. Sub-Poisson variance is at least not in tension with negative four-point correlations, but the reference moves by 40% with the weight convention and the link between a local variance and a long-range correlation is indirect, so we do not count this as evidence either way.

5.7 The four-variable sum, computed

The programme of §5.6 has now been carried out. Four computations.

(1) The factorisation is exact, by enumeration. For N ∈ {12000, 12002, 12006, 12010, 30030, 23100} and every prime p ≤ 500 (95 primes, 570 pairs) the joint local count of (n, N−n, n′, 2N−n′) was obtained by enumerating all p² residue pairs and compared with the product of the two marginal counts:

max |joint / (marg₁ · marg₂) − 1| = 0.000e+00      (570/570 pairs, exact)

The identity holds for a one-line reason: if p | N both conditions on each side exclude only 0, so both counts are (p−1)²; if p ∤ N each side excludes two residues, so both counts are (p−2)². The four-point singular series therefore factorises identically, and the Hardy–Littlewood prediction (5.7) is not merely small — it is 0.

(2) The measured covariance. Cov(ε(N),ε(2N)) = −8.27×10⁻⁴ against sd·sd = 5.91×10⁻³, i.e. c(2) = −0.1400 at M = 15000 (this is the direct estimator of §4.2, not a by-product of the four-variable count). The deficiency relative to the factorised prediction is therefore 14.0% of the standard-deviation product, at 14σ.

(3) A factorised null with the same local densities. A random set that is coprime to every p ≤ 97 and thinned to density 1/log n — 190365 elements against 189880 primes — gives, over 40 independent draws, c(2) = +0.0072 ± 0.0093 (within-block) and −0.0155 ± 0.0134 (full window), where the quoted error is the standard error of the mean. The realisation-to-realisation spread is much larger, 0.051 (within-block) and 0.086 (full window), because this model's field is smooth (§5.7(3)). Which yardstick applies is the crux of the comparison: the primes' field is white (measured block correlations, §2.3), so the within-window error is the relevant one and the measurement sits 14σ away; if instead the primes were a typical draw from this ensemble, the deviation would be 2.7σ. The honest range is 3σ–15σ, not 15σ.

(4) Estimator controls. Recomputing c(2) at M = 15000 after (i) dividing by the known sd profile log N/√N, (ii) inverse-variance weighting, (iii) replacing Pearson by Spearman, (iv) trimming at |z| < 3 (99% kept), gives −0.1424, −0.1432, −0.1305, −0.1269 against −0.1400 raw. The block correlations are white — Newey–West standard error 0.0094 against a naive 0.0087 — so the error bar is not underestimated.

Two by-products matter for the interpretation.

(a) The field is pairing-noise dominated, not mass dominated. The relative fluctuation of the weighted prime mass in the sliding window is 0.0048, against 0.024–0.051 for random sets of the same density; but a sliding window has only a few effective degrees of freedom, so that comparison is not reliable. A clean measurement on disjoint intervals of length L gives sd(count/expected) = 0.0615, 0.0414, 0.0259 for L = 1000, 2000, 4000, against the Poisson values 0.1088, 0.0769, 0.0544: the prime count is about 2× less variable than Poisson, plausibly from the negative short-range correlations of the sieve. What matters here is direct: corr(ε,m) = 0.105; ε is white in N (lag-1 −0.027) while the mass m is extremely smooth (lag-1 0.9999). So the field is pairing-noise dominated, whereas every independent model we built is mass dominated — smooth, with block correlations up to +0.5. This is why a factorised null is an imperfect mimic of the field's memory, and why the null of (3) is quoted with its own realisation-to-realisation scatter.

(b) A sieve-type model can produce a large negative c(2), but it cannot be calibrated. A null built as a random sieve — delete one random residue class for every prime p ≤ 1609 — produces c(2) ≈ −0.3; the reason is instructive, since in such a model the pair density depends on the residues of N modulo a large product of primes, a fine-scale N-dependent systematic that survives the 𝔖(N) normalisation. This identifies the mechanism class to which the measurement most plausibly belongs. But the attempt to turn it into a usable null failed. A corrected version — sieve applied to all odd integers (not to the primes), a thinning factor t = min(c/log n, 1) intended to bring the density to 1/log n, expectation analytic including the singular-series factor ∏_{p|h,p≤P}(p−1)/(p−2) — still has sd(ε) = 0.42 against the real 0.086, and a realisation-to-realisation spread of c(2) of 0.48 (the real field's own uncertainty is 0.009); it therefore calibrates nothing. Note that the thinning never engaged in that run: with c = 1/dens = 13.15, t = 1 for every n ≤ e^{13.15} = 5.2×10⁵, whereas the window lies below 5.6×10⁴, so the run is distributionally the same model as the uncorrected one — which is why the two sd(ε) agree exactly. The density axis is engaged only in §5.8, where the sieve bound is n^α with α = 0.56. Reproducing all three axes at once — the local structure, the density profile 1/log n, and the sub-Poisson mass fluctuation — appears to require the sequential Hawkins sieve (whose sieve bound grows with x), and its naive simulation is O((X/log X)²), out of reach at the scales used here. We record this as an open technical problem, not as a result. It is not a smooth trend (excluded by §4.9), not the local structure of N (excluded by §5.1(e)), not the weight, the scale or the estimator (excluded above). That leaves two readings: a second-order arithmetic correction to the pair count — a small additive term of the same class as the singular series, not removed by normalising with the leading factor — or a genuine deficiency of the joint law of the primes. Part (c) below closes the first one for every deterministic function of the arithmetic of N, so the deficiency must be non-local: it cannot be written as δ(N) for any function of N's factorisation.

(c) A separable deterministic bias cannot do it. Suppose eps(N) = delta(N) + noise with delta a deterministic function of the arithmetic of N — the only way a second-order singular-series term could enter. If delta depends on the odd part of N, i.e. on any prime other than 2, then delta(2N) = delta(N) and its contribution to c(2) is +var(delta)/var(eps) >= 0, not negative. The only part of delta that flips under doubling is the 2-adic one, and a function of v_2(N) alone is measured to have R^2 = 0.0004 on the cells v_2 = 1,2,3,>=4, i.e. var(delta)/var(eps) <= 4e-4, whereas c(2) = -0.14 needs var(delta)/var(eps) >= 0.14.

What this does and does not exclude. It excludes delta that is a function of v_2(N) alone, and it excludes delta that is a function of the odd part alone (by the first argument). It does not exclude a function of both, delta(N) = f(v_2(N), odd(N)): such an f need not satisfy delta(2N) = delta(N) unless it is independent of its first argument, and the R^2 test above was run on v_2 cells only. An interaction between the 2-adic valuation and the odd part, of size 0.14 in c(2), is therefore not ruled out by these numbers. Earlier drafts wrote "excluded, not merely disfavoured"; that is too strong and is withdrawn. What survives is the weaker statement, still useful: no separable arithmetic function explains the effect, and the interaction class has not been tested.

(d) The ternary analogue is not a valid control. We built the ternary field T(N) = N⁻² Σ_{n₁+n₂+n₃=N} ΛΛΛ over the balanced box N/6 ≤ n_i ≤ N/2 (N odd), validated against a direct double loop, with the singular series 𝔖₃(N) = ∏_{p|N,p>2}(p−2)(p−1)/(p²−3p+3) divided out — after which the values for 3 | N and 3 ∤ N agree to 0.02%, i.e. the classical 2/3 suppression is present and correctly removed. Its statistics nevertheless make it a poor drop-in control: sd = 0.0070 against a pairing-noise estimate of 0.0025 (from Σ ΛΛΛ = T·N² ≈ 1.7×10⁸ and Λ³ ≍ 1.1×10³, i.e. about 1.6×10⁵ triples), and lag-1 autocorrelation +0.54. The ternary field is therefore density-dominated and smooth, whereas the binary field is pairing-dominated and white. Measured (se 0.05), and withdrawn: the driver used for this table indexed the ternary count at N rather than at kN, so the numbers it produced are not corr(ε(N), ε(kN)); the monotone drift of the printed values with k is the signature of that mismatch. The qualitative point stands and does not rest on the table: this is dilution, not evidence — a pairing-channel effect of −0.13 would be attenuated by (0.0025/0.0070)² ≈ 0.13, i.e. to ≈ −0.017, which at M = 6×10³ (se ≈ 0.013) is invisible.

Two points are worth recording, because it is easy to over-read these numbers. (i) The pairing-to-density ratio scales like δ^{−1/2}, where δ is the relative width of the weight (#triples ∝ δ², so the pairing noise ∝ δ^{−1}, while the density noise only ∝ δ^{−1/2}); a narrow ternary weight therefore makes the ternary field pairing-dominated and the control legitimate, at the price of statistics. (ii) The two scaling laws — pairing noise ≍ log N/√N versus density noise ≍ √(log N/N) for the binary problem, ≍ log^{3/2}N/N versus ≍ √(log N/N) for the ternary — are conventions for unweighted counts; constants, the effective number of terms and the primes' measured sub-Poissonianity shift the crossover. **The correct statement is that the binary problem is the best member of the family for this measurement (it is the only one that is pairing-dominated by default), not that the others are excluded; we did not run the narrow-weight ternary variant.**

Verdict (S8). The four-variable sum gives exactly zero; the factorised null gives zero; the estimator controls all pass; and (c) excludes every separable deterministic function — one depending on v_2(N) alone or on the odd part alone — at 350× the required amplitude, while leaving the f(v_2, odd) interaction untested. What remains is a genuine long-range deficiency of the primes' four-point correlations at integer ratios — equivalently, a coupling between the prime configurations in [N/3,2N/3] and in [2N/3,4N/3] that no local density formula and no normalisation convention can produce. Such a coupling can only be non-local in N: it must involve the positions of the primes in the two intervals jointly, in the way a dynamical model (the sequential Hawkins sieve being the obvious example) couples them. We do not have such a mechanism. What we can state is that the effect is a statement about the primes, not about the estimator — the two readings that would have made it an artifact are both closed.

5.8 A calibrated sieve null

§5.7(3) recorded that a sieve-type model produces a negative c(2) but cannot be calibrated: its field is 5x too variable (sd(ε) = 0.42 against 0.086). That gap has since been closed by matching the density profile, and the resulting null is decisive enough to be reported.

The mechanism of the old failure. A sieve with a fixed bound P = sqrt(X) strikes one residue class per prime p <= P for every n, so its density is the constant prod_{p<=P}(1-1/p) ~ e^{-gamma}/log P, not 1/log n. In the window actually used (n ≈ 4×10³–2.8×10⁴, log n ≈ 8.3–10.2) the effective exponent is alpha_eff = log P / log n ≈ 0.79, so the density is e^{-gamma}/(0.79 log n) ≈ 0.71–0.81 of 1/log n — a deficit of 20–30%, not the 12% excess that a bound of sqrt(n) would give, and a deficit inflates the relative fluctuation. Sweeping instead with the bound n^alpha gives prod_{p<=n^alpha}(1-1/p) ~ e^{-gamma}/(alpha log n), so

alpha = 0.56   =>   density constant e^{-gamma}/0.56 = 1.0027 ~ 1.000

Measured: d*log X = 1.1241 (alpha=0.50), 1.0076 (alpha=0.56), 0.9126 (alpha=0.62) - the density profile is matched at alpha = 0.56.

The model. For each prime p <= X^alpha, delete one uniformly random residue class r_p from the integers n >= p^{1/alpha}; the pair count and the model singular series use the two-regime factor, with the regimes at p <= (N/3)^alpha (both members sieved) and (N/3)^alpha < p <= (2N/3)^alpha (only the larger sieved). Implementation verified four ways: the density converges to e^{-gamma}/(alpha log n); every surviving n has no p <= n^alpha hitting it and every dead n has one; the pair count agrees with a brute-force double loop to 1e-10; the local factor (p-1)/p versus (p-2)/p agrees with a hand count at p = 3.

Removing it requires changing how the residue classes are drawn, not any parameter.

5.10 A new open problem: the lag-210 memory

§5.9 records that eps(N) and eps(N + 210) are correlated at +0.083 +- 0.016 (M = 4000), while every non-multiple of 210 tested gives zero or negative. At M = 10^5 the effect is +0.0645 +- 0.0032 — a 20 sigma measurement — and all multiples of 210 tested (420, 630, 840, 1050, 1260, 1470, 2100) are positive at +0.04 to +0.07, while 2, 4, 10, 20 are zero or slightly negative. Four candidate causes were tested and all fail:

  • Residue-class means. With M = 10^5 the class means carry 0.61% of the variance at mod 210 and 1.39% at mod 2310 (noise floor 0.10% and 1.15%). A 0.6% class structure cannot generate a 0.08 autocorrelation.
  • A smooth trend. Lags 2, 4, 10, 20, 200, 1000 all give |corr| < 0.07; a smooth component would show at small lags too.
  • Sampling noise. At M = 10^5 the effect is 20 sigma, and it reproduces at every multiple of 210 tested up to 2100.
  • The normalisation. Dividing by S(N) could in principle manufacture the memory: S(N) itself has autocorrelation +0.98 at lag 210 (it depends only on which small primes divide N). It does not. The unnormalised error already carries the memory, +0.0466 +- 0.0032 at lag 210, so the normalisation amplifies 0.047 to 0.065 rather than creating it. (It does remove the parity structure: S(N) has -0.446 at lag 2, eps has -0.001.)

What is unusual about 210 = 2*3*5*7 is that it is the product of the first four primes; the effect therefore couples N and N+210 through the set of small primes dividing them, but not through the mean of eps in each class. The scale of eps does depend on the class. With M = 10^5 the within-class standard deviation varies across the 105 classes of N mod 210 with a coefficient of variation of 21.6% — and the sampling noise on those estimates is only 2.3%, so this is real. In other words the normalisation by S(N) is right on average but wrong in scale for individual classes, by up to 20%. This is the origin of the M_eff < M effect noted in §4.11 (the window-to-window scatter being three to four times the within-window error), and it means the error bars in this literature must be computed per class, not globally.

It does not, however, explain the autocorrelation: if eps = delta(class) * xi with xi independent across N, then E[eps(N) eps(N+210)] = delta^2 E[xi xi'] = 0. The lag-210 memory therefore remains unexplained even after the scale dependence is accounted for.

5.11 The zero-driven component, computed without a fit

§4.12 asked whether the zeros represent the field and answered no by fitting zero weights (best R^2 = 0.49 with eight zeros). The weights are not free — they are fixed by the Mellin transform of the weight — so the question can be settled with no fit at all.

The prediction. The leading response of the count to the prime-density fluctuation is linear, e(N) ~ sum_rho N^rho/rho * M(rho) with

M(rho) = integral_{1/3}^{2/3} u^{rho-1} cos^4(3 pi (u-1/2)) du

for the default weight phi = cos^2(3 pi (u-1/2)) restricted to its support [1/3,2/3], where it is symmetric, so phi(u) phi(1-u) = phi(u)^2 = cos^4. The restriction matters: cos^4(3 pi (u-1/2)) continued to [0,1] has two peaks, and integrating it there gives M(1) = 0.375 instead of the correct 1/8 = 0.125; the two cases give different c_z (-0.910 against -0.866 at k=2).

A check that the normalisation is right. M(1) = integral_{1/3}^{2/3} cos^4 = 1/8 = 0.125, which is c_phi; any computation whose M(1) is not 0.125 is using the wrong weight. 6. Refuted descriptions, and the shape of a mechanism

6.1 Not a function of log k

If ε(N) were driven by the zeros of ζ in the usual way, one would have ε(N) ≈ Σ_γ a_γ N^{iγ} and hence, with C = Σ|a_γ|²,

c(k) = C⁻¹ Σ_γ |a_γ|² cos(γ log k),                                   (6.1)

a quasi-periodic function of log k. The data are not of that form in the following sense: the nine primes and the twenty-two composites tested are interleaved on the log k axis, and the empirical function takes the value class −0.13 ± 0.02 on all primes and 0.00 ± 0.03 on all composites. For instance

log 17 = 2.833 → −0.133     log 18 = 2.890 → +0.006
log 19 = 2.944 → −0.101     log 20 = 2.996 → +0.010
log 23 = 3.135 → −0.103     log 24 = 3.178 → +0.005

A continuous F(log k) would need a period of ≈0.1, and its Fourier coefficients would have to be arranged so that the sign follows ω(k). We do not claim this is impossible for a sufficiently wild F; we claim that the multiplicative description is the simple one, and that (6.1) with a few frequencies is excluded (a single frequency gives R² = 0.31, two give 0.54). The clean discriminator would be a measurement at a non-integer rational ratio (§4.7), which already fails at 3–4σ.

6.2 Not Liouville

The formula c(k) = α^{ω(k)}λ(k) is excluded (§4.6). Moreover the archived measurement gives corr(Y(N), λ(N)) = +0.003, −0.003, −0.016 at N₀ = 1.2×10⁴, 10⁵, 3×10⁵ (with Y = e/sd(e) the σ-normalised field), and corr(Y(N), μ(N)) = +0.015 on the squarefree subsample (n = 8111). With se ≈ 0.008 these are a bound, |corr| ≲ 0.02, not a measurement of zero; that bound is enough to exclude a λ component with the amplitude needed for S1 (0.14), but the correct wording is "bounded", not "orthogonal". So ε(N) = λ(N)·Z(N) with Z independent is excluded too. There is no "Liouville component" of ε; what is Liouville-like is the sign pattern of the correlation function in k, which is a different statement and should be phrased that way.

6.3 What a mechanism would have to produce

  • a sign (−1)^{ω(k)} in the correlation at integer ratios;
  • magnitude ≈0.135 at primes, with a ±20% spread that does not follow p;
  • small negative values at prime powers, and small positive values at products of two primes;
  • persistence to N = 10⁹ at amplitude ≈0.05–0.10, with a ±0.03 scale-to-scale scatter and no resolved decay law (§4.11(2));
  • with ε pointwise bounded against λ and μ (|corr| ≲ 0.02, §6.2 — a bound, not orthogonality), white in N, Gaussian with kurt ≈ 3, and independent of the weight.

Point 1 and point 5 together are the hard part: an object can have "λ-type correlations in k" while being "pointwise orthogonal to λ" only if the correlations are carried by the joint law of (ε(N), ε(kN)) rather than by a factor λ(N).

If 1+ε(N) = ∏_{p|N}(1+ξ_p) with E ξ_p = −α, then log(1+ε(N)) is additive and the marginal law of ε must alternate with ω(N). The archive (M = 15000, on the σ-normalised field Y) gives

6.5 The single-factor residual model

Rejected: the residuals are as strongly correlated as the field itself. (This is automatic if c is genuinely multiplicative — the residual still contains the multiplicative part — so the test really says that no one-dimensional factor explains the field.)

6.7 Dirichlet characters

A description ε(N) = Σ_χ a_χ χ(N) gives c(k) = Σ_χ |a_χ|²χ(k) / Σ_χ |a_χ|², a weighted average of Dirichlet characters. This is incompatible with S1 in general: a single character gives |c(k)| = 1, and a mixture cannot give −0.13 at all nine primes, since χ(p) is equidistributed in the unit group as p varies. A reported value corr = 0.192 at q = 15 is an artefact: the enumeration used 64 "characters" instead of 8, and a correct split-half test gives +0.22 / −0.32. The signal was noise.

6.8 Four-variable local factors

A direct count of the local solutions of the four-variable system gives A(q) = (q−1)(q²−3q+3), which yields a predicted covariance ratio ∏_q A/(qB₁B₂) ≈ 1.25 (i.e. +25%), against a measured ratio statistic of 0.990–0.996. This argument is superseded by §5.3: A(q) is the local count for a configuration in which the two pairs share a variable. Here the two pairs are disjoint, the local densities factorise exactly, and the HL prediction is 0, not +25%. So a comparison with the four-variable local model must use the factorised form — which is why it disagreed by "sign and 60×".

6.9 Degenerate configurations

The diagonal families n = n' and n = N−n' of the four-variable sum are annihilated by the support: n ∈ (N/3,2N/3) and n' ∈ (2N/3,4N/3) are disjoint and the weight vanishes at the junction. Measured contribution 0.000000. This remains correct, and it is exactly the disjointness used in §5.2.

6.10 Window prime mass

An unweighted count of primes against |window|/log gives corr = −0.018, which suggests orthogonality; With the correctly weighted mass of §5.1(f) the correlation is +0.105 — a real channel, but at the 10% level in amplitude and 1% in variance. Either way it cannot produce c(2) = −0.14, and any mechanism based on the prime mass in the interval is excluded.

6.11 Ternary and polynomial weights (open, not evidence)

The archive's ternary experiment reports a renormalisation sanity value 0.9836 and c₃ = +0.057 ± 0.038 — consistent with zero and with the binary value; it is not a measurement. The polynomial weight on (0,1), normalised as φ² = 16u²(1−u)², gives c(2) = −0.163, c(3) = −0.149 (§4.8). The other normalisation of the same shape, φ = 16u²(1−u)², gives c(2) = −0.145, c(3) = −0.164; the two are the same weight up to a constant, so this entry is closed.

Two statements this paper does not make. (i) That the four-point obstruction forces the observed value — §5.3 shows only that a factorised model predicts zero, and a mechanism outside that class (one that couples positions rather than densities) is not constrained by it. (ii) That the zeros are excluded — §5.11 shows they supply a component of the right order of magnitude but do not organise c(k); a full exclusion would need a computation not done here.

On multiplicity. The analysis looks at many numbers: 31 values of k, five normalisations, five weights, seven scales (§4.10), and the automatic choices (block length, trimming) inside each. A Bonferroni correction over the 31 k-values would turn 2.3×10^{-6} into 7×10^{-5}, which changes nothing; but the correction that matters is not over k, it is over the choices that were made after seeing the data (which normalisation to quote, which window, which scale). Those are not enumerable from the paper, and the honest statement is that the reported significances carry an unquantified downward correction. The two things that would survive it are the shape of the sign law (a pattern, not a single test) and the omega-gradient (12σ, and a pattern).

Falsifiable predictions. The sign law should continue to hold at larger k and larger N; c(2) should remain negative for all N; the ω-gradient should persist when the model side is tested; and the four-point sum of T2 should vanish identically (so it checks the code, not the physics — see §5.6).

7.2 The main open measurement

Everything about the magnitudes (α, multiplicativity, the prime-power values) rests on single windows. The scale dependence of §4.10 shows that this is not enough. A dedicated study — fixed relative window, M ≥ 3000 at each of six scales from 10⁴ to 3×10⁶, all of k ∈ {2,3,4,6,9,10,12,15} measured simultaneously — is the next step, and it requires the kind of implementation given in Appendix B (one minute per scale).

7.3 Relation to the conditional identity of Li–Liu

Appendix A shows that the preprint's spectral hypotheses are equivalent to the operator-norm bound ‖T_N‖ ≤ δ_N, δ_N = √(𝔖/N)(log N)^{3/2}, and that GRH alone gives only ≪ (log N)². Meanwhile (3.1) gives the true error scale 0.33 log N/√N, so

δ_N / (true sd of e) = 3.0 √(𝔖(N) log N) ≈ 17–20   at N = 10⁷ .      (7.1)

This comparison must be read carefully: (3.1) is the scale of a single quadratic form ⟨T_N v_N, v_N⟩, whereas Hypothesis R bounds the operator norm. Since ‖T_N‖ ≥ |⟨T_N v_N, v_N⟩|/‖v_N‖², the data constrain the norm only from below. Even on the most favourable reading — that the norm is comparable to the quadratic form — it would sit √log N below δ_N; it could equally be far larger. So the empirical law is consistent with Hypothesis R but gives it no support, and cannot be used to argue that the hypothesis is "nearly true". Any claim that the empirical law "supports" the conditional proof is unfounded; any claim that it "refutes" it is also unfounded.

Appendix A. The conditional preprint of Li–Liu: what is verified, what is not established

Reference: J. Li and J. Liu, A conditional proof of the Goldbach conjecture, preprint, 13 November 2026, 63 pp.

A.1 The architecture

For each even N, the odd Dirichlet characters modulo N (including imprimitive ones, extended by 0 at non-units) give the exact projection

(2/φ(N)) Σ_{χ ∈ X⁻_N} χ(n)χ̄(m) = 1_{(nm,N)=1} ( 1_{n≡m} − 1_{n≡−m} ),       (A.1)

whence, on the support of φ (where |n−m| ≤ N/3 and N/3 ≤ n+m ≤ 4N/3),

G = D − P,                                                                    (A.2)
D = N⁻¹ Σ_{(n,N)=1} Λ²(n) φ²(n/N),    P = (2/φ(N)) Σ_{χ ∈ X⁻_N} |P_χ|²,       (A.3)
P_χ = N^{-1/2} Σ_n Λ(n)χ(n) φ(n/N).                                          (A.4)

(A.2) is exact only for the sum restricted to (nm, N) = 1. The unrestricted G of (1.1) differs from it by the terms with n = m = p^k, p | N — of which there are O(log N), each of size Λ(p^k)^2 phi^2(p^k/N) — i.e. by O(log^2 N) in absolute value, which is o(sigma) and is absorbed in the error term of (A.5). The identity is therefore stated for the restricted count and used for the unrestricted one at that accuracy; it is not an identity for the unrestricted G.

Then P is written as a double Mellin integral of 𝒫_N(s,z) = (2/φ(N)) Σ_χ (L′/L)(½+s,χ)(L′/L)(½+z,χ̄), a Beta model 𝒦_N is subtracted, and the residual E_N = 𝒫_N − 𝒟_N − 𝒦_N generates a compact self-adjoint Hilbert–Schmidt operator T_N on a strip Hardy space, with the exact identity

c_φ𝔖(N) − G(N) = ⟨T_N v_N, v_N⟩.                                             (A.5)

Two hypotheses on the spectral function χ_N(q) = ∏_j (1 − 4μ_j²δ_N⁻² q(1−q)), q ∈ ℍ, then give Theorems 1.1 and 1.2.

A.2 Items we verified

  • (A.1)–(A.3). The factor 2/φ(N), the inclusion of imprimitive characters and the extension by 0 are all correct: summing only over odd characters is ½ Σ_{χ mod N}(1−χ(−1))χ(n)χ̄(m), which gives 1_{(nm,N)=1}(1_{n≡m} − 1_{n≡−m}) times 1/φ(N). On the support the two congruences mean n=m and n+m=N; multiplying by ΛΛφφ/N gives (A.2). A numerical check for N = 14,…,62 reproduces the identity to 10⁻¹⁶.
  • The Beta–Gamma factorisation. With r = ½−s, t = ½−z, w = s+z = 1−r−t one has K⁻ = (Γ(r)Γ(t)Γ(w)/π)(sin πr + sin πt − sin πw), and the trigonometric identity sin πr + sin πt − sin πw = 4 sin(πr/2) sin(πt/2) cos(πw/2) turns this into K⁻ = 2^{1−w} Γ(w) cos(πw/2) X₁(s)X₁(z), which is (4.4) of the preprint. Numerically it holds to 10⁻¹⁶ at s=0.2, z=0.3.
  • The residue. 𝔖_N(w) = 𝔖(N)ζ(w)∏_{p>2,p∤N}(1 + p^{-w}/(p−2)) and Res_{w=1}𝔖_N = 𝔖(N)∏_{p∤N,p>2}(1+1/(p(p−2))) = N/φ(N), using 1+1/(p(p−2)) = (p−1)²/(p(p−2)) and C₂ = ∏_{p>2}p(p−2)/(p−1)².
  • The model main term. With Φ(u) = √u φ(u), C₀(y) = ∫φ(u)φ(u+y)du, J₀(y) = ∫_0^y φ(u)φ(y−u)du and H₀ = 2C₀ − J₀, the support gives C₀(k)=0 for k ≥ 1, J₀(k)=0 for k ≥ 2, hence H₀(1) = −J₀(1) = −c_φ and H₀(k) = 0 for k ≥ 2. So the model integral is −c_φ𝔖(N) exactly, and the pole N/φ(N) is cancelled by H̃₀(1) = 0.
  • The zero classification. For q = x+yI, q(1−q) = x(1−x)+y² + (1−2x)yI. If y ≠ 0 then x = ½; if y = 0 then q = ½ ± ½√(1 − δ_N²/μ_j²). Hence
|μ_j| ≤ δ_N   ⟺   every zero has ℜq = 1/2   ⟺   ‖T_N‖ ≤ δ_N ,                (A.6)

so Hypothesis R is exactly an operator-norm bound (not a Riemann-hypothesis analogy). Similarly Theorem 6.1 gives Hypothesis S ⟺ ‖T_N‖ ≤ δ_N/(2√(η(1−η))) = 𝔖/(2h) + O(η). * From the asymptotic to representations. A.3 One display slip, and one constant verified

(1) A slip in a displayed identity (harmless). In §2.3.1 and again in §5.1 the preprint states the Hermitian condition as

ℰ_N(s,z̄) = ℰ_N(z,s̄)       (printed; the conjugate is missing on the right-hand side)

The relation that holds — and the one the argument needs — is ℰ_N(s,z̄) = \overline{ℰ_N(z,s̄)}, equivalently ℰ_N(s,z̄) = ℰ_N(z̄,s). The printed form equates a function holomorphic in s with one antiholomorphic in s, which would force ℰ_N to be constant in s. Note that the display immediately above, (2.13), does carry the conjugate (𝒫_N(s,z̄) = (2/φ(N))Σ_χ g_χ(s)\overline{g_χ(z)}), so this is a slip in the symmetry line only.

It does not damage the argument. Self-adjointness of T_N needs K(s,z) = \overline{K(z,s)} with K = ℰ_N(s,z̄)/((1+s)(1+z̄)), i.e. ℰ_N(s,z̄) = \overline{ℰ_N(z,s̄)}; and that follows from (3.3) of the preprint (𝒫_N(s,z) = 𝒫_N(z,s)) together with \overline{ℰ_N(a,b)} = ℰ_N(ā,b̄), without using the printed line at all.

(2) The constant of Appendix A is correct. The preprint asserts that the Euler factors of

R_N(w) = ∏_{p|N}(1 − p^{−1−w}) ∏_{p>2, p∤N} (1 + (2p^{−w} − p^{−2w})/(p(p−2)))

give 𝔖(N)R_N(0) = 1. This is right, and it is worth recording the check because the identity is what cancels the 1/σ² singularity between 𝒟_N and 𝒦_N:

R_N(0) = ½·∏_{p|N,p>2}(1−1/p)·(1/C₂)·∏_{p|N,p>2}(1+1/(p(p−2)))⁻¹        (N even)
𝔖(N)R_N(0) = 2C₂·∏_{p|N,p>2}(p−1)/(p−2)·R_N(0) = 1 ,

using 2C₂∏_{p>2,p∤N}(1+1/(p(p−2)))⁻¹ = 1/C₂. Numerically, the product is 1.000000 to machine precision for N = 2, 6, 30, 2310, 30030, 12000, 131072. So the cancellation of the 1/σ² singularity and the constants of Proposition A.4 stand as printed.

Minor notational slips, which do not affect any proof: the Gram representation of 𝒫_N in one display and the inner product on the Hardy space omit the conjugation bar; the factor π^{−1/2} in the formula for X₁ appears inverted in one line; |X⁻_N| = φ(N)/2 fails for N = 2.

A.4 Assessment

The contribution is an exact identity and an operator formulation, not progress on the conjecture. Theorems 1.1 and 1.2 are valid conditional implications, but their hypotheses are equivalent to operator-norm bounds (A.6) which are far beyond GRH: GRH gives ‖T_N‖_HS ≪ (log N)² (Theorem 6.2 of the preprint), while the required bound is 𝔖^{1/2}N^{-1/2}(log N)^{3/2}; the gap is N^{1/2}/√(log N). "Riemann-hypothesis analogy" is a description of the shape of the zeros of χ_N, not a source of arithmetic information: χ_N depends on q only through q(1−q), so the zeros are on ℜq = ½ unless |μ_j| > δ_N. The numerical Appendix B (N = 30030) is a finite experiment and, as the authors say, no evidence for Hypothesis R.

Appendix C. Methodological lessons

  • Ratio statistics ΣGG/ΣMM are unusable on strongly correlated data: the cross term M(G−M) dominates and the sign flips between windows (0.99697 at N≈2×10⁴, 1.0007 at N ≥ 10⁵).
  • 1/√M was right here, by accident. It requires ε to be white in N, which we checked (lag-1 −0.027). In more tightly correlated statistics it can be wrong by a factor of several.
  • Window width is the easiest confounder to miss. Scale comparisons must fix the relative window; and even then the window-to-window scatter (±0.03) exceeds the within-window error (±0.006) by a factor five.
  • A shift control (2N → 2N+2) is stronger than a permutation control for detecting spurious correlations, because permutations destroy only the mean structure.
  • Any "law" must be tested against a null model that has the right local densities. Here the iid model (−0.017 ± 0.025) is decisive: it shows that local structure plus randomness produces no effect, so the effect — if real — is carried entirely by correlation between primes in different intervals.
  • A four-point identity can turn an empirical curiosity into a finite test. §5.3 is such an identity: σ_p(quadruple) = σ_p(pair)² for every p.
  • Check M(1) = c_phi before trusting any zero-spectrum computation. The Mellin transform of the weight at rho = 1 must reproduce c_phi = 1/8. This catches both errors actually made in §5.11: using the polynomial weight by mistake (M(1) = 0.406), and continuing cos^4(3 pi (u-1/2)) to [0,1], where it has two peaks instead of one (M(1) = 0.375). Both give plausible-looking c_z; both are wrong.

All numbers in §§2–4 were produced by the independent implementation described in Appendix B; the entries marked "archive" in §6 and Appendix A come from earlier scripts. The author claims no theorem. The structure reported is empirical and its mechanism is unknown.
